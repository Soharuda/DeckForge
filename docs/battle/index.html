0.31.3

The deck preview, restyled as the arena

The screen before a game wears the board's clothes now: a plum mat under framed translucent panels, gold section headers over a fading rule, the commander as a hero row with a gold-framed thumbnail, a quantity only when there is more than one, set and number at the end, and a breathing blue Play button.

The empty band at the top is gone rather than filled. The title, the deck's name and Close moved into the head of the side panel, the list runs the full height, and one inset pass per screen carries the bars, the cutout and the left and right gesture strips, with a 20dp floor at every edge for the phones whose camera sits under the glass and reports no cutout at all. The list fades at both ends and the panel's middle scrolls, so the pre-cache button stops being the thing that falls off the bottom.

Two bugs on the way: a commander deck said "commander" twice, once for the format and once for the board, and the right panel clipped its own content. The layout is the same in either orientation, side by side when the window is wide and stacked when it is not.

0.31.2 A land is played by the rules, not by the offer

A dragged land was told it could not be played while the dock's own Play land button played the same land a second later. The drag was judged against the list the seat offered at the moment it was asked; lands now go down whenever the engine's own land count still allows one, and the offer stops being the rule for them. The hand also became one gesture over the row instead of one per overlapping card.

0.31.1 A drag out of the hand lands

Playing a card is a drag - you pull it out of the hand and let go over your half - and the drag was the thing that did not work. The drop was decided by arithmetic about the board: your half of the screen, down to a hand band guessed at 96dp from the bottom. A card lifted out of the hand and let go again landed in the strip that arithmetic still called the hand, so it went nowhere and said nothing, which is what "I cannot even play a basic land" looks like from the outside.

The line a card has to cross is the hand's own top edge now, measured by the hand itself in the same coordinates your finger is read in, or a finger's worth of lift above wherever in the fan you picked it up. Either one counts, so it does not matter whether you grab a card by its title or its bottom edge: carry it up and let go, and it goes down. Playable cards glow and lift, the dock says "Release to play it" while the card is clear of the hand, and a card dragged back down is still a card put back.

The dock's own Play land / Cast button works again as well. It was grey for every card in every game, because nothing ever set the card that button acts on and a touch on a card in hand only opened it. A touch now picks the card up as well as opening it, so the button carries the card's name and plays it from there - a second door for a card you have already looked at. A land that cannot go down says why rather than quietly showing you its picture.

0.31.0 One board

The switch in the top bar is gone. Battle Mode has one board now: the draft's, the one that has been the default since 0.30.2 and the one every card, pile and rank on it was rebuilt for. The old three-lane table, its seat stages, the band along the bottom you played from and the depth model behind them are deleted rather than left behind a toggle - nine hundred lines of a board nobody opens. What the switch remembered is no longer read, because the choice it offered only has one side left.

The corner column is the only corner the app draws, so the arithmetic in its test is the app's: your library at the foot, the graveyard standing on it, the general above that, three cards and his word sized from the near half of the window so nothing is ever drawn over the rank beside it. An empty graveyard keeps its place in the column instead of the column closing up.

Two faces of the same card are finally one card. Sea Gate Restoration, Bala Ged Recovery, Tangled Florahedron and Shatterskull Smashing are a sorcery or a creature by type with a land on the other face, so every one of them sat in hand looking unplayable and the land drops they were meant to be went unused - the AI's as well, since it decided what was a land by asking the card's types.

So the seat stops guessing at all. It asks the engine's own getPlayable for the list of what it may play or cast, so a cost reducer, an alternative cost, a treasure or a modal face counts the way the engine counts it, and the land drop is the engine's count rather than a marker kept beside it that could drift. Both faces answer to their own ids, so the land you tapped for is still the card in your hand. 94 battle tests green, including a new one that plays a deck of two-faced lands and waits for the land face to actually arrive on the table.

0.30.3 An arena on the draft board

0.30.3 is the draft board wearing a restyle, five rounds of it, over the board rather than in place of the table. The playmat runs plum to maroon with dark veins, the midline seam is brighter and breathes, and each seat carries its colour on the edge of its cards: yours blue, theirs red, a card you can act on lit cyan and lifted in the hand. Lands and artifacts of one name merge into a card with a count badge, as Arena does it; creatures never merge, and a tap on a merged card goes to an untapped copy. A rank counts tapped cards wider so a rank of tapped lands stops overflowing, and the life orbs are a ring in the seat's colour with the total inside. Your general wears a gold edge.

The command zone became a framed column at the left edge of each seat, drawn flat over the tilt so it can never be clipped: your library at the foot, then the graveyard, then the general nearest the midline, and theirs hanging from the top-left the same way. The slot keeps its place when the general is on the battlefield and shows its tax as a badge. With the draft board on, the board is the whole screen and the top bar floats over it rather than holding a row.

Playing a card from the hand is a drag on either board now: the card follows your finger, its slot fades, your half of the table lights up and letting go is the play. A tap only opens the card, except in an instant window, where a drag answers. Cards drop onto the table on a spring, playable cards breathe in the hand, the hand re-fans as it changes, and a permanent that dies scorches to char and its ash curves into that seat's graveyard.

Two of those needed the engine rather than a guess. Deaths come from a watcher that records every permanent that went to a graveyard, so a bounce or an exile is no longer mistaken for a death; and the tax badge reads the commander play count the engine itself charges from, after finding that the event it had been counted on is never raised in this port.

0.30.2 one phase pill, corners that hold what they hold, the draft first

The phase rail is gone: five segments that shifted the whole bar every time the turn moved into the next phase are now one pill of a fixed size whose word changes, with every slot around it fixed too.

The pile corners are measured for everything standing in them - the graveyard and exile chips were drawing over the lands lane, and on the draft board the deck came up under the general's card. Deck, general and rank now have a place of their own.

And the draft board is the default, once: a phone that had "table" saved opens on the draft and remembers its own choice from then on.

0.30.1 the board that was working, back

0.30.0 came up wrong on the phone - the table with no cards on it, the draft with nothing but hands and its information out of place - so all of its board work is reverted: the measured sizes, the command zone folded into the back rank, the tap that only opened a card, the bar across the top, and the turn-one draw that came with them. What is in this package is 0.29.0's board, the one that was seen to work, with the version raised so it installs over 0.30.0.

The pieces worth keeping come back one at a time, each one watchable on the phone before the next goes in.

0.30.0 A board measured to the screen, a tap that only reads

0.30.0 is the draft board grown up. Every size on it was written down for one phone - a 72dp column for the command zone, 40dp libraries, a 64dp card in the hand - so on a short landscape window the gutter ate a fifth of the width and on a wide one the board floated in the middle of nothing. The hand, the pips, the libraries, the spacing and the lift that plays a card are now shares of the window, bounded at both ends: a short screen still shows a card, a tablet gets a table rather than a bigger hand.

The command zone went with it. Your general is the first card of your back rank and your library is the last, so neither is drawn over a card any more; the row shares itself out instead of leaving a margin guessed for it. Their general stands at the left of their row the same way.

One gesture does one thing now, on both boards. A tap opens a card whole, and the card carries the play - Cast, or Play land - when it can be played. A lift out of the hand plays it. Before this a tap on a land in your hand played it outright and a tap on an instant was the cast itself, so the only way to read a card was to risk playing it. The general in the command zone is played from its own card now, which is the only door it has.

The bar along the top is a bar: on the very top edge, its height a share of the window, holding the far seat's life pip and nothing else. Neither seat has a portrait to draw, so the avatar disc and the name above the table are gone, and the number is the same pip the draft stands at the end of your row.

And commander draws on turn one. The engine applies the two-player tournament rule, where the player who goes first skips the draw step of their first turn, and commander counts as two-player as far as it is concerned. A commander table does not play that way, so every seat draws.

0.29.0 The right opponent, and the bar out of the way

0.29.0 is two fixes and one addition. The seat across the table is now built for the format your deck declares: hand it a commander deck and it sits down with a hundred-card singleton deck of its own, general in the command zone and all; hand it sixty cards and it answers with sixty. Before this, one fixed sixty-card deck was seated whatever arrived, so a commander deck played a game of forty life and a command zone against an opponent who had neither.

The turn and phase bar has left the strip under the top bar. It is a compact pill on the right of the centre row now, over the one band of the table that holds no cards, and the board has the height it was spending. Your life total still rides in it, except while the draft is drawing its own orbs at the ends of the row.

And the draft has a command zone: your general stands at the left of your back rank, breathing when the engine says it can be cast, and a tap on it casts it. The opponent's general stands at theirs, so a commander game looks like one.

0.28.0 A second table, behind a switch

0.28.0 is a draft of the board that did not come from this app, kept beside the table rather than put in its place. One real perspective plane with the cards laid out flat inside it, a purple playmat, a glowing midline, two ranks a side, life orbs at the edges and a fanned hand. It is a different board and not a restyled one: no lane along the bottom, no card sheet on a long press, no dragging a card out of the hand and onto the table.

So it is a switch in the top bar, reading Table or Draft, and it remembers where it was left. Turned on, the draft draws the same game: the same permanents, tapped where they are tapped and lit where the table is asking a question, and a tap on either board answers through the same code. The game is untouched; only the hand that deals it out differs, which is the point of having both for a while.

One correction had to be made before the two could be compared. The draft hinges its plane at the middle, and a middle hinge draws the board at nine tenths of the height it was given: at forty degrees and a far edge at four fifths, a tenth of the table is a band of nothing above it. Hinged at the near edge instead, which is also the edge that has to stay on the bottom of the screen, every rank fills its slot and the near edge is drawn at the size it was measured.

0.27.0 The table owns the whole screen

0.27.0 is about the space above the cards. The app's own title bar used to stay up over the board while a game was on, and the board's top bar still reserved the status bar's inset even though the bars are hidden, which together was a quarter inch of nothing above Log and Leave. The title bar belongs to the lobby; it goes as soon as the cards are out, and the top bar now keeps clear of the cutout only.

The opponent's side was also the small half of the table. Half the board's layout draws as a little less than half the screen, because the far half is further away and draws shorter, so an even split gives them their share of the top. The far edge is drawn at seven eighths rather than four fifths and the lean is twenty degrees rather than twenty-two: still a table you are sitting at, without their lands and artifacts squeezed into slivers. The band along the bottom takes a quarter of the height and the top bar is 36dp, so the two halves are what has the room.

0.26.0 One table, leaning away from you

0.26.0 is about where things are. The board used to be two halves that each leaned about their own middle, which is two planes rather than one: their half tilted toward you as much as yours tilted away, so their board read as if it were lying on your side of the table. It is one plane now, leaning away about its near edge. The edge in front of you is drawn at the size it is measured, the far edge at four fifths of that, and the eye's distance is worked out from those two numbers. Both seats are laid out on the one plane, so their board is further away because it is further away.

Your side is the row you sit at and the rank in front of it. The deck is in the near left corner drawn as card backs with the count of what is left on top, and the graveyard is stacked directly over it. The lands are in the row along the bottom between the deck and your hand, your hand is fanned in the middle of the screen, and your artifacts and enchantments are in the same row to the right of it. In front of that row, filling your half up to the middle of the table, is your front rank: creatures, planeswalkers and battles, at the largest size the height allows.

Theirs is the same shape seen from across the table. Their lands and artifacts run along their own back edge, their hand is face down between them, and their front rank faces yours across the midline, all of it drawn smaller and higher because it is further off.

The band along the bottom no longer comes and goes with your hand, because it is not only your hand any more: a row with a deck and a graveyard in it stays where it is. The one panel that asks a question stands over the near right corner of the table instead of taking a row, and the rank behind it keeps clear of it rather than hiding underneath.

0.25.0 Cards that were never missing

0.25.0 fixes a refusal rather than a gap. The pre-game check named cards the engine cannot build, and some of those names were the check's fault. The sets register plenty of cards under the plain-letter spelling, while DeckForge sends the name as Scryfall prints it, marks and all, so "Hamato Ninpō" did not match "Hamato Ninpo" and a card the engine implements was reported as missing. Names are now compared with the accent taken off both sides, so "Lim-Dûl's Vault" and "Lim-Dul's Vault" are one card, and so are "Æther Vial" and "Aether Vial".

The pool itself was already whole and is still current: the sets are XMage's as of November 2026, 592 of them, 94,263 printings, none of which fail to build.

history Upright to choose, sideways to play

0.25.0 puts the phone the right way up for each half of the job. The decklist is a column of cards, so the menu is portrait: the deck's name and its pictures at the top, the whole list down the middle, and Play under a thumb. Pressing it turns the phone and hands over to the table, which is sideways because two halves and a hand need the long side of the screen. Leaving a game turns it back.

The turn over is not a cut. The deck's cards riffle off the stack, the last one grows until it is the whole screen, and it crossfades into the table, which is already being dealt underneath it. The back of that card is drawn rather than downloaded, so the animation starts on the tap instead of waiting on a network.

The game moves too. Cards drop into their slots and the hand is dealt one card after another. A tapped permanent rotates and shrinks into its slot rather than jumping. Life counts to its new total and the disc knocks as it changes. A spell slides onto the stack, the phase rail lights the phase being played with a wash of brass, and every new turn announces itself. Everything the table is asking you to pick breathes, so a legal target can be found on a full board.

history The table

The table is the screen Arena taught us to expect. It is landscape, and it is a table: their half across from you, yours in front, creatures nearest the middle where combat happens, the decks and the graveyards in the corners, and your hand with the cards you can actually play lit and the rest left quiet.

A card is played the way a card is played. Drag it out of your hand; while it is under your finger the card you would be playing is drawn at table size, and your own half lights up if it can be played there. Let go over your half and it is played, let go anywhere else and it slides back into your hand with a line at the bottom saying what is missing. Tapping a card no longer plays it, because tapping is how you read.

One tap on any card, in hand or on the table, puts the card itself up on the right at the size it is printed to be read, and a tap anywhere puts it away again. No dialog, no separate oracle panel: the printed text is the text.

The dock in the corner only appears when the engine wants something, and it asks in a sentence. Questions better answered by touching the table say so and get out of the way. Tapped permanents turn a quarter turn, creatures carry their power and toughness, tokens are marked, and the strip between the halves is the turn's phases, or the stack when something is on it.

history Before the game

A deck used to be dealt the moment it arrived. Now there is a lobby. It checks the deck against the cards this phone can build, and while it does it says so: the name of the card it is on, how many of how many, and a bar that moves. Anything the engine cannot build is listed by name with a reason, instead of the deck quietly arriving with holes in it.

Then the decklist, in full, with the sideboard and the commander, and the number of pictures it will need. Pressing on asks for those pictures ahead of time: every card at its printing, the illustration as well as the whole card, the tokens the deck's own wording implies, and the other seat's deck, with progress on screen while it runs. The first game therefore starts with pictures already on disk rather than during the first turn.

0.16.0 One land a turn

0.16.0 fixes a bug worth naming. A land drop could be used twice in a turn, which is how a Mountain and an Exotic Orchard both appeared on turn one. The seat now asks the engine's own count of land drops as well as its own marker, and only plays a land the engine offered it in that main phase. A test plays an all-lands deck turn after turn and calls a second land directly, the way the phone did.

history The pictures

Card art comes from Scryfall, once. Every card in the deck is resolved to its printing and its name, and both the whole card and its illustration are fetched while the game is being dealt, a few at a time and spaced out. They are written to a small index on disk with the pictures themselves, so the second game with the same deck asks the network for nothing at all.

Tokens are fetched ahead of time too, by reading the deck's own wording: a card that says it creates a 1/1 white Soldier makes "Soldier" into a warm-up request before the token exists. A token nobody predicted still gets its picture the moment it appears, and is remembered after that.

Nothing about a game depends on the network. Pictures arrive; the table plays either way.

history What the table already does

More than one creature can block the same attacker, and the AI piles on by itself when that is the only way to kill something bigger than anything it owns. Instants are cast at instant speed. A deck that arrives with a commander is played as a commander game: forty life, the general in the command zone, and its tax tracked by the engine.
